# Save Stevie: long-term replayability ideas

Design notes from our discussion. These are possibilities to explore, not implemented features or a committed release roadmap. Names, numbers and unlock requirements are provisional.

## The goal: enjoyable after the 500th run

The ambition is for someone to still want to play three years from now, after they have unlocked everything. Achievements, rewards and new content can bring people back, but enduring depth comes from interesting decisions, meaningful combinations and skills worth mastering.

The central question: **After everything is unlocked, can a familiar encounter still make an experienced player change their plan—and can they explain why their decision worked?**

Save Stevie's distinctive hook is drawing, alongside the question: “What ridiculous thing can I make my pencil do this run?” Build around that rather than relying only on escalating stats or a growing checklist of unlocks.

## Shape techniques unlocked outside normal rewards

Player idea: drawing shapes creates special effects. Unlock techniques through achievements or other progression instead of making each technique a normal run-reward ability.

Original examples:

- Draw a circle to create a lava hole, spending more ink than an ordinary wall.
- Draw a square to create a turret lasting roughly ten seconds.
- Eventually explore more complicated drawings. A unicorn that bursts into confetti was a playful example, not a serious requirement.

### A small drawing language with many interactions

Use a few recognizable rules:

| Layer | What it determines | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shape | Structure or function | Circle: hazard; square: temporary turret; triangle: projectile redirector |
| Equipped ink | Behavior | Fire circle burns; Gravity circle gathers enemies; Frost circle slows |
| Pencil trait | Cost and tradeoff | Cheap, short-lived structures; expensive, durable ones; stronger structures that consume their boundary walls |
| Placement and timing | Whether the decision pays off | A strong lava hole in the wrong place still wastes ink |

Potential loadout rule: unlock a broad collection, but bring only two shape techniques into a run. Choose them alongside pencil traits and inks so builds retain strengths and weaknesses rather than eventually granting everyone the same enormous toolbox.

Recognition should be forgiving. The challenge is when and where to draw a triangle, not convincing the game that a slightly messy triangle counts.

Explore a handful of shapes with many interactions before adding complicated drawings. Three shapes interacting with several inks and different enemy behaviors could create more depth than twenty unrelated spells.

## Five foundations for lasting depth

### 1. Different strategies solve different problems

- Turrets handle scattered enemies but are vulnerable to erasers.
- Hazards punish crowds, while fast monsters cross them quickly.
- Redirectors handle projectiles but cannot stop a charging monster.
- Each build needs situations that require improvisation.

Avoid universally best inks or synergies. The overly strong Electric and Gravity builds demonstrated how an effect that solves everything removes decisions.

### 2. Enemy combinations change the problem

Interesting encounters require more than combining a fast enemy and a tank. A monster that shelters others, paired with one that attacks from behind that shelter, creates a problem to solve.

Basil is a useful starting point because he changes what other monsters do. Explore enemies that interact with one another and respond differently to structures. This creates variety without requiring hundreds of monster species.

### 3. Ink has competing good uses

Ink buys immediate safety, damage, temporary structures or preparation for an upcoming attack. Preserve decisions like:

> “I can afford the turret, but then I might not be able to return the boss's shot.”

Unlimited regeneration that lets players draw everything continuously would undermine this tension. Costs and structure lifetimes should keep placement and timing meaningful.

### 4. Runs require adaptation

Let players begin with a plan, then adjust it in response to rewards, encounter combinations and optional risks. Provide enough choice for decisions and enough uncertainty to prevent the exact same winning recipe every run.

Useful random encounters might offer a powerful pencil with a flaw, require defending a second target, exchange an ink, or trade a harder upcoming encounter for a specific reward.

### 5. Skill matters beyond raw difficulty

Create room to improve at efficient strokes, directing enemy routes, structure placement, boss returns and ink management.

Extra HP and faster enemies are useful challenge settings, but also explore modifiers that change decisions:

- Turrets attract enemies.
- Enemies learn to avoid hazards.
- Returned projectiles restore ink while ordinary regeneration is reduced.

Challenge mode should reveal and reward those skills.

## Post-victory challenge mode: the detention board

Player proposal: unlock challenge mode after the first defeat of the wave-20 boss. Customizers could include extra enemy HP, faster enemies and other difficulty adjustments.

Expand this into selectable restrictions with rewards: one effect slot, expensive ink, tougher bosses or accelerated enemies. Named modifier combinations become recognizable challenges to master.

Prefer a mix of numeric modifiers and changes to how players solve encounters. Do not assume every possible combination will be balanced or enjoyable.

## Additional reasons to return

| Idea | Experience and reward |
| --- | --- |
| Stevie's homework assignments | Optional objectives: defeat a boss using returned shots, win with only closed shapes, or protect three doodled classmates. Earn stamps and artwork. |
| A new notebook each run | Choose a page with a benefit and complication: graph paper rewards corners; tracing paper lets certain attacks pass through walls; sticky notes create small defendable islands. |
| Monster report cards | Collect annotations such as “Basil stole my lunch AGAIN.” Discover behaviors, complete species-specific challenges, and unlock alternate doodles or animations. |
| Weekly shared homework | Everyone gets the same seeded run and starting kit. Compare completion, Stevie's remaining HP and ink efficiency. |
| Sideways upgrades | Unlock alternate pencil traits, strange utilities and ink variants with meaningful tradeoffs, instead of endlessly increasing starting power. |
| Victory keepsake pages | Save a page showing the build, key synergies and Stevie's handwritten account of the run. |

## Stranger ideas that fit Stevie's notebook

### The margins fight back

A suspicious door, lunchbox or badly drawn treasure chest appears in the margin. Spend ink to circle it and activate an optional encounter when your defenses are ready. Stevie may find a reward—or discover that he drew teeth on the lunchbox.

### Stevie misremembers your last run

After a victory, Stevie draws an exaggerated account: “MY PENCIL SHOT A MILLION LIGHTNINGS.” On the next run, accept that story as a challenge with a related starting effect and drawback. Past victories generate invitations to play differently.

### Rescue yesterday's doodle

A defeated run leaves a lost page recording its build and the monster that ended it. Attempt a short rescue encounter using that build. Winning makes the page a keepsake and gives a frustrating death a satisfying follow-up without requiring another full campaign.

### The teacher corrects the page

Optional encounters introduce readable red-pencil corrections: a marked zone will be erased, a line is stamped “TOO MESSY,” or a wall must be redrawn before a countdown ends. Clear warnings make this a playful drawing-skill test.

### An accomplishment-grown doodle pet

Stevie has a terrible drawing of a pet. Specific achievements add rabbit ears, roller skates, a lightning tail or Basil's fancy fork. Players assemble an increasingly absurd portrait through play. Keep it mostly cosmetic so collection does not upset balance.

### Pass the notebook

Share a challenge code containing a seed, modifiers and starting build: “Can you survive my horrible pencil?” Codes could provide personal challenges without requiring accounts or servers initially.

## Progression principles

- Achievements introduce new systems; the systems need to remain interesting after all unlocks are earned.
- Consider eventually capping permanent stat upgrades so opening waves do not become chores for experienced players.
- After those caps, let scraps unlock new ways to play and things to collect.
- Optional encounters should change decisions or builds, not merely interrupt play with another reward.
- Preserve distinct loadouts, real weaknesses and scarce ink.
- No system guarantees a three-year lifespan. Test whether familiar content keeps producing new decisions.

## Suggested exploration order

The initial return-to-play proposal was detention challenges, homework objectives and victory keepsake pages: mastery, variety and attachment. Page types and margin encounters could supply later surprises.

For deeper long-term design, first prototype a few shapes with ink interactions, test recognition and costs, and check whether enemy combinations force different uses. Keep that exploration separate from a promise to ship every idea above.
